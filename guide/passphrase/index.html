Protecting your saved data

What the passphrase encrypts, what it deliberately leaves readable, and the one thing that cannot be undone.

Grispy writes what you save to a file inside your browser's profile directory on your own computer. You choose whether that file is encrypted. Without a passphrase it is ordinary readable text; with one, the values and the names you gave your forms and presets are sealed behind a key only you can produce.

The offer, once

The first time you ever save anything, Grispy asks:

Protect your saved data? Grispy stores what you save in a file on this computer. A passphrase encrypts it, and you enter it once each time you start your browser. It cannot be recovered if you forget it.
Set a passphrase Type it twice — deliberately, because with no recovery path a typo would lock you out of your own data permanently and without any signal.
Not now Declines. Grispy will not ask again, and you can turn encryption on later from Settings whenever you change your mind.

Set one and Grispy confirms: Saved data is now encrypted on this computer.

Unlocking, once per browser session

With encryption on, the first time Grispy needs your saved data after you start your browser it shows this instead of the usual screen:

This page has saved data Enter your passphrase to use it. There is no way to recover it if it is forgotten.

Enter it once and Grispy stays unlocked for the rest of that browser session. The passphrase never leaves your computer and is never sent anywhere. The key it produces is held in your browser's memory for that session alone — it is never written to disk — and it is gone once you close the browser.

To drop it early without closing the browser, use Lock now on the Encryption tab of Settings.

Four limits, stated plainly

These are the same four the privacy policy states, and they are worth reading before you rely on any of it.

1. If you decline, nothing is encrypted

Your saved data is stored as ordinary readable text, and anything that can read files on your computer — other software running under your user account, another person on the same unlocked machine, or someone who takes an unencrypted laptop — can read it. Your operating system's full-disk encryption and a locked screen are then what protect it.

2. Encryption conceals what you saved, not which sites you saved it on

Grispy has to know whether a page has anything saved for it without asking for your passphrase on every page you visit — otherwise you would be prompted constantly, nearly always on pages with nothing saved. The list of addresses it holds data for is therefore left readable. Someone with access to the files on your computer could see which sites you have saved forms for, and how many forms and presets you have, without being able to read any of the values inside them.

3. A forgotten passphrase cannot be recovered

Not by you, and not by us — we never have it. Your options at that point are an export you took while unlocked, or resetting Grispy, which deletes everything and starts you over. Resetting works even when you cannot unlock.

This is the reason to take a readable export and keep it somewhere you trust. A readable export is the only way back into your data if you forget your passphrase — and it is readable by anything, so where you put it matters.

4. Uninstalling deletes it

Removing the extension removes its storage. Export your data first if you want to keep it.

Changing your mind later

Everything below is on the Encryption tab of Settings. The tab always opens with a status line saying where you stand:

Saved data is not encrypted.No passphrase set.
Encrypted · unlocked for this browser sessionSet, and in use now.
Encrypted · lockedSet, and not yet entered this session.
Set a passphrase Turns encryption on, including after you declined the first offer. Everything saved is encrypted with it.
Lock now Forgets the key for this session. The next thing that needs your data asks for the passphrase again.
Change passphrase Asks for the one you use now, then the new one twice. Everything already saved stays readable — Grispy rewrites one small value rather than re-encrypting every record, so this is quick however much you have stored.
Turn off encryption Asks to confirm, then asks for your passphrase. Everything saved goes back to being readable by anything with access to this computer.

Choosing a passphrase

  • Long beats complicated. Several unrelated words you will remember beat a short string of symbols you will not.
  • Do not reuse one you need elsewhere. There is no recovery here, so the failure mode is different from a website password you can reset.
  • Write it down somewhere real, or keep it in your password manager. This is not the advice given for online accounts, and the reason is the same one: nobody can reset this for you.
  • Take a readable export before you rely on it. See limit 3 above.
